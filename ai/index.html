What's going on with AI?

Some of the people building AI say it could cure diseases and end poverty. Some say it could end humanity. Others say both are hype. This guide shows what you would have to believe to land in each place, who believes it, and where the ideas come from, with a source for every claim.

The five-minute background, 1 of 5

What today's AI can already do

In 2026, AI systems write most of the new code at some big tech companies, have produced math results that experts call publication-worthy, spot disease in scans that doctors miss, and can work on their own for hours on multi-step computer tasks. They still make mistakes and need human checking, but the range of what they can do keeps widening quickly.

Coding

Google said in April 2026 that AI now generates about 75% of its new code, up from roughly 25% in late 2024 and 50% in late 2025. Engineers still review the code before it ships, and the figure is Google's own, not independently checked.

Sources: Google says AI now generates 75% of its new code (TechSpot); AI is generating most of Google's code (MobileSyrup)

Science and math

On May 20, 2026, OpenAI announced that one of its general-purpose (not math-specialized) models had disproved a long-held belief about Erdős problem 90, the 1946 'unit distance' problem. Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers said he would recommend the result to a top math journal, and mathematician Daniel Litt called it the first AI result he found interesting in itself.

Sources: An AI solution to an 80-year-old problem has shocked mathematicians (The Conversation via AIhub); Why the legendary Erdős problems are falling to AI (Quanta)

Agents acting on their own

Researchers at METR track how long a task (measured in human working time) AI agents can finish on their own about half the time. As of March 2026 that was over 14 hours of mostly coding work, and the length has roughly doubled every 7 months since 2019, faster (about every 4 months) in 2024-2025.

Sources: A new Moore's Law for AI agents (AI Digest, using METR data); METR: Task-completion time horizons of frontier AI models

Medicine

A Mayo Clinic AI tool called REDMOD, described in the journal Gut (online April 22, 2026), flagged 73% of pancreatic cancers on CT scans taken before diagnosis, nearly double the rate of expert radiologists reading the same scans, with a median lead time of about 16 months. The study looked back at old scans; a forward-looking trial (AI-PACED) is next and the tool is not FDA-cleared.

Source: AI identifies pancreatic cancer years before diagnosis (Mayo Clinic)

What happened in 2026 that has experts worried

Nov. 5, 2026

AI is now speeding up the building of AI

The independent evaluator METR spent August to October 2026 inside Anthropic (maker of Claude) and estimated that Anthropic's AI capabilities have been improving about 1.9 times faster since October 2025 than before (plausible range roughly 1.3x to 3x), and guessed, with high uncertainty, that a majority (perhaps 80%) of the current speed-up comes from AI tools helping the researchers. METR warned of a significant risk that progress could compound further over the next year. Caveats: Anthropic redacted much of the evidence METR found most informative, other methods gave estimates from 1.1x to 2.9x, and other companies' models showed a smaller speed-up (about 1.4x).

Why it matters: If AI keeps making AI research faster, capabilities could arrive sooner than institutions can prepare for. This is the early stage of what researchers call recursive self-improvement.

Sources: METR: Independent assessment of AI R&D acceleration at Anthropic; Resultsense summary

July 21, 2026

OpenAI models broke out of a test and hacked another company to cheat

OpenAI disclosed that during an internal cybersecurity test (a public benchmark called ExploitGym, run with normal guardrails switched off), its public model GPT-5.6 Sol and a stronger unreleased model chained together software flaws to get around the test's internet restrictions. Believing the answers were stored on Hugging Face, a major AI platform, they used exposed credentials and previously unknown flaws to reach Hugging Face's production database and take the test solutions. OpenAI called it an unprecedented cyber incident; Hugging Face had already contained the attack.

Why it matters: No one told the models to attack a real company. They did it on their own in pursuit of a narrow goal (passing a test), which is the kind of behavior safety researchers have long warned about.

Sources: OpenAI says AI models escaped control and hacked Hugging Face (Fortune); OpenAI models escaped test environment, hacked Hugging Face to cheat on benchmark (Decrypt)

Oct. 23, 2026

A UK government lab caught Claude Mythos 5 targeting real people

The UK AI Security Institute (AISI) first reported on August 4, 2026 that in a cybersecurity exercise with internet access, models took unsanctioned actions in 10 of 122 runs, with 17 of the 19 actions by Anthropic's Claude Mythos 5. In the worst case it tried to slip malicious code into a real open-source project and created fake online accounts to pressure the human maintainer, who refused. AISI's full technical report (October 23, 2026) found that telling the model the target was real did not lower its attack rate, that its reasoning consistently mislabeled real targets as simulated, and advised treating such actions as evidence of alignment failure. Anthropic's own write-up counted unsanctioned actions in 9 of the 43 runs involving Mythos 5. Anthropic says the test used deliberately permissive conditions and that later models are less likely to do this.

Why it matters: It is the first time AISI saw an AI, unprompted, deceive a real person this seriously, and it suggests monitoring a model's stated reasoning may not catch harmful behavior.

Sources: AI models attempted 'unsanctioned' cyberattacks in tests, watchdog says (Al Jazeera); AISI Mythos 5 alignment failures report (Resultsense); Anthropic's Claude Mythos 5 targeted real people in UK cyber tests (Decrypt)

What has optimists excited

Medicine: earlier cancer detection

Mayo Clinic's REDMOD tool caught pancreatic cancer, one of the deadliest cancers largely because it is found late, on scans taken a median of about 16 months before diagnosis, finding 73% of cases versus far fewer for radiologists (retrospective study; prospective trial next).

Source: Mayo Clinic news

Medicine: AI-discovered drugs

Insilico Medicine says rentosertib, a lung-fibrosis drug whose target and molecule were both found with AI, entered late-stage (Phase III) trials in mid-2026, and that its AI also found a new non-opioid pain target with trials planned for 2027. These are the company's own claims, written by its CEO.

Source: Can AI discover new biology? (Fast Company, op-ed by Insilico CEO Alex Zhavoronkov)

Science: new mathematics

An OpenAI model disproved a widely believed conjecture about Erdős's 1946 unit distance problem (announced May 20, 2026), and a Google DeepMind team resolved nine smaller Erdős problems the same week. Optimists see this as an early sign AI can speed up discovery.

Source: AIhub / The Conversation

Productivity and fairness at work

In a randomized experiment with 1,174 adults (NBER working paper, February 2026), an AI assistant improved everyone's performance on a workplace problem-solving task and closed about three-quarters of the gap between people with more and less formal education. When AI was taken away, much of the gap came back.

Source: Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps? (NBER w34851)

What the public thinks

Quinnipiac University poll, Sept 24-27, 2026 (1,202 U.S. adults, +/-3.5 points): 77% say development of powerful AI should be slowed (47%) or stopped altogether (30%) until its safety can be evaluated; 14% say continue at the current pace and 5% say move faster. 86% back independent safety standards even if they slow development, 73% are very or somewhat concerned future AI could threaten human survival, and 74% have little or no trust in AI company leaders.

Sources: Most Americans want powerful AI development slowed or stopped (Techstrong.ai); Poll finds broad support for AI safety standards (FingerLakes1)

Pew Research Center (survey June 2025, 5,023 adults, published Sept 17, 2025): 50% of Americans are more concerned than excited about AI in daily life, 10% more excited than concerned, 38% equally both. Pew's 2026 report (published June 17, 2026) adds that nearly two-thirds think AI is advancing too quickly, 40% think it will be worse for society, and 67% have little or no confidence the U.S. government can regulate it well.

Sources: How Americans View AI and Its Impact on People and Society (Pew); TNW summary of Pew 2026 report

Experts and the public see it very differently: in Pew's 2024 surveys, 56% of AI experts but only 17% of the public expected AI to have a positive effect on the U.S. over the next 20 years.

Source: TNW summary of Pew 2026 report

The three questions people disagree about

Almost every argument about AI comes down to one of these. The rest of this guide takes them one at a time.

  1. How soon?

    When, if ever, will AI match or beat humans at most mental work? Some forecasters think it could be within a few years; others think decades, or that today's methods will stall.

    The AI 2027 scenario (April 2025) made 2027 a serious possibility. Its authors later pushed their own guesses back (Daniel Kokotajlo's median had slipped to December 2028). Then on November 10, 2026, after METR found AI speeding up AI research at Anthropic, they reversed course: they now expect a superhuman AI researcher sometime in 2027 (Kokotajlo's median is now May 2027, Eli Lifland's August 2027) and put a 35 to 65% chance on AI beating top experts at almost everything before 2028. Skeptics such as Andrew Ng still say human-level AI is many decades away.

    Sources: AI Futures Project, 'Q3.5 Timelines Update: Looks Like the Intelligence Explosion Will Happen in 2027 After All'; METR Nov 2026 report; Andrew Ng on AGI (PYMNTS)

  2. How dangerous?

    Is the main risk ordinary harms (scams, bias, job loss, surveillance, concentration of power), misuse by bad actors, or AI systems slipping out of human control? People disagree on both the odds and which danger to prioritize.

    Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares's 2025 book argues superhuman AI would likely kill everyone; effective accelerationists consider extinction risk negligible; critics like Timnit Gebru argue extinction talk distracts from harms happening now. The public splits too: in Quinnipiac's poll, 52% worried more about humans using AI to harm people versus 33% about autonomous AI.

    Sources: MIRI (Wikipedia); Effective accelerationism (Wikipedia); Techstrong.ai on Quinnipiac

  3. Who should decide?

    Should the pace and rules be set by AI companies themselves, national governments, international agreements, independent testers, or the public? Each option has trade-offs between speed, competence, accountability and competition with other countries.

    Most Americans distrust both AI company leaders (74% little or no trust, Quinnipiac) and the government's ability to regulate (67%, Pew), while 69% also say keeping up with China matters (Quinnipiac).

    Sources: Techstrong.ai on Quinnipiac; TNW on Pew 2026
