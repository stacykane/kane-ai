Background

Where the ideas come from

The fear that AI could wipe us out and the hope that it could make us immortal did not appear out of nowhere. Both grew out of a small set of overlapping communities, many of them in Silicon Valley and Oxford. Knowing that history helps explain why the debate sounds the way it does. It does not, by itself, tell you who is right.

A short history, 1965 to now

1965

I.J. Good imagines an 'intelligence explosion'

British mathematician I.J. Good, who had worked with Alan Turing on wartime codebreaking, published 'Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine.' He argued a machine smarter than people could design even better machines, setting off an 'intelligence explosion,' so it would be 'the last invention that man need ever make,' as long as it stayed controllable.

Source: Technological singularity (Wikipedia)

1988-2006

The Extropians

Philosopher Max More and collaborators launched Extropy magazine in 1988 and later the Extropy Institute, a hub for optimistic transhumanist ideas: radical life extension, cryonics, nanotechnology, mind uploading and AI. Their 1990s email list was an early meeting place for many future AI-risk and tech figures. The institute closed in 2006, saying its mission was essentially complete.

Source: Extropianism (Wikipedia)

1993

Vernor Vinge names 'the Singularity'

Mathematician and science-fiction writer Vernor Vinge presented 'The Coming Technological Singularity' at a NASA-cosponsored symposium. He predicted greater-than-human intelligence sometime between 2005 and 2030, after which the human era would end in ways we cannot forecast.

Sources: Technological singularity (Wikipedia); Vinge's essay (SDSU mirror)

1999-2024

Ray Kurzweil popularizes the Singularity

Inventor Ray Kurzweil's bestsellers, especially The Singularity Is Near (2005), predicted human-level AI by 2029 and a merger of humans and machines around 2045. He reaffirmed those dates in The Singularity Is Nearer (2024). His optimistic version made the idea mainstream in Silicon Valley.

Source: Technological singularity (Wikipedia)

2000-2009

Eliezer Yudkowsky, the Singularity Institute, and LessWrong

Eliezer Yudkowsky founded the Singularity Institute for Artificial Intelligence in 2000, at first to speed up AI. By 2005 he had concluded advanced AI could be extremely dangerous, and the group shifted to AI risk (renamed the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, MIRI, in 2013). In 2009 he launched LessWrong, a blog about clearer thinking that grew into the 'rationalist' community. His 2025 book with Nate Soares, If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies, argues superhuman AI would be fatal.

Sources: MIRI (Wikipedia); LessWrong (Wikipedia)

2005-2024

Nick Bostrom and Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute

Philosopher Nick Bostrom founded the Future of Humanity Institute at Oxford in 2005 to study 'existential risks' that could wipe out humanity or permanently curb its potential, including AI. It became the academic home of longtermist and AI-risk thinking. Oxford closed it on April 16, 2024, after what the institute called growing administrative headwinds.

Source: Future of Humanity Institute (Wikipedia)

2007-2011

GiveWell and the birth of effective altruism

Former hedge-fund analysts Holden Karnofsky and Elie Hassenfeld founded GiveWell in 2007 to find the charities that do the most good per dollar, starting with global poverty and health. Toby Ord started Giving What We Can in 2009, and the name 'effective altruism' (EA) was coined in 2011. EA later overlapped heavily with the rationalist community and turned growing attention and money to AI risk.

Source: Effective altruism (Wikipedia)

2014

Bostrom's 'Superintelligence'

Bostrom's book Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies (Oxford University Press, July 2014) argued a smarter-than-human AI would be very hard to control and that even a harmless-sounding goal could lead it to grab resources and resist shutdown. It became a bestseller and was praised by Elon Musk, Bill Gates and Sam Altman, bringing AI extinction risk into elite conversation.

Source: Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies (Wikipedia)

2015

OpenAI is founded

On December 11, 2015, OpenAI launched as a nonprofit with $1 billion in pledges from backers including Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Reid Hoffman and Peter Thiel, promising to develop AI to benefit humanity as a whole rather than shareholders. Worries about AI risk were part of the founding story; the company later created a for-profit arm and, along with rivals like Anthropic (founded 2021 by former OpenAI staff), now builds the most advanced systems.

Source: Introducing OpenAI

2017-2022

Longtermism and 'What We Owe the Future'

Oxford philosophers William MacAskill and Toby Ord coined 'longtermism' around 2017: the view that improving the long-term future is a key moral priority. MacAskill's 2022 book What We Owe the Future brought it to a mass audience, arguing that because vastly more people could live in the future than ever have, reducing extinction risks (including from AI) matters enormously.

Source: Longtermism (Wikipedia)

2022 (November)

FTX collapses

Crypto exchange FTX collapsed in November 2022. Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, was a prominent effective altruist and major funder of EA and AI-safety causes, and his FTX Foundation shut down. He was convicted of fraud in 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in prison on March 28, 2024. The scandal badly damaged EA's reputation and prompted soul-searching about 'ends justify the means' reasoning.

Sources: Effective altruism (Wikipedia); FTX founder sentenced to 25 years (Axios)

2022-2023

Effective accelerationism (e/acc)

A loose online movement called effective accelerationism appeared in a May 2022 newsletter by pseudonymous authors including 'Beff Jezos,' revealed by Forbes in December 2023 to be physicist Guillaume Verdon. It argues that speeding up technology, especially AI, is the best way to solve humanity's problems, opposes regulation, and treats AI doom as negligible. Investor Marc Andreessen's 'Techno-Optimist Manifesto' (October 2023) carried similar ideas into the mainstream.

Source: Effective accelerationism (Wikipedia)

2023-2024

Gebru and Torres coin 'TESCREAL'

AI ethics researcher Timnit Gebru and philosopher Émile P. Torres coined TESCREAL in 2023 (Transhumanism, Extropianism, Singularitarianism, Cosmism, Rationalism, Effective Altruism, Longtermism), arguing these overlapping ideologies share roots in 20th-century eugenics and drive the race to build AGI. Their paper 'The TESCREAL bundle' appeared in the journal First Monday on April 14, 2024, arguing AGI is too ill-defined to make safe and that the field should build well-scoped tools instead.

Sources: The TESCREAL bundle (First Monday); TESCREAL (Wikipedia)

2023-2024

Pushback on the TESCREAL label

Critics of the framing, including some who are not fans of Silicon Valley, say it lumps together movements that often disagree. Eli Sennesh and transhumanist sociologist James Hughes (Institute for Ethics and Emerging Technologies, June 2023) called it a left-wing conspiracy theory that ignores the progressive strands within these movements. Writer Ozy Brennan (Asterisk, June 2024) argued someone can think AI is dangerous without holding the other beliefs, that critics treat thought experiments as plans, and that the critique offers little concrete guidance on AI policy. Philosopher Seth Lazar has called the term polemical (2026).

Sources: Conspiracy theories, left futurism, and the attack on TESCREAL (IEET/Medium); The 'TESCREAL' Bungle (Asterisk); TESCREAL (Wikipedia, criticism section)

The big idea behind much of the worry: longtermism

You will hear this word a lot. Here is the argument in plain terms, and the main objection to it.

Longtermists start from three ideas: people who will live in the future matter just as much as people alive now; there could be vastly more future people than have ever lived; and what we do now can affect whether they get to exist and how well they live. Put together, they argue that preventing human extinction (from pandemics, nuclear war, or uncontrolled AI) may matter more than almost anything else, because extinction would erase all of those future lives at once.

About 117 billion people have ever been born, including the roughly 8 billion alive now; about 109 billion have lived and died. Both numbers carry large error margins. The future figure is one illustrative scenario from Our World in Data (about 800,000 more years at a steady 11 billion people), not a prediction. Chart redrawn for this guide from those figures.

Sources: The future is vast: Longtermism's perspective on humanity's past, present, and future (Max Roser, Our World in Data); How Many People Have Ever Lived on Earth? (Population Reference Bureau); Longtermism (Wikipedia)

The main objection

Critics say that once you multiply tiny chances by astronomically large numbers of future people, almost any present suffering can be made to look small by comparison, so the argument can justify neglecting poverty, injustice or climate harms happening to real people now, and even justify extreme measures in the name of the future. Philosopher Émile P. Torres argued this in Aeon (October 2021), pointing to Bostrom's description of even terrible catastrophes as 'mere ripples' in the bigger picture. Others object that we cannot predict the far future well enough to act on it, that it rests on contested ideas in population ethics (Kieran Setiya's 2022 Boston Review critique of MacAskill), and that it resembles 'Pascal's mugging' (being swayed by a tiny chance of a huge payoff). Longtermists reply that many actions, like pandemic preparedness, help both present and future people.

Sources: Against longtermism (Émile P. Torres, Aeon); The New Moral Mathematics (Boston Review review of What We Owe the Future); Longtermism (Wikipedia, criticism)

People who changed lanes

  • Geoffrey Hinton. Spent decades building the neural-network methods behind today's AI, then left Google in 2023 so he could warn about its dangers freely. Source
  • Daniel Kokotajlo. Worked on governance at OpenAI from 2022 to 2024, then quit after losing confidence that it would behave responsibly around the time of AGI, refusing to sign a non-disparagement agreement that put about $2 million in equity at risk. Source
  • Elon Musk. Co-founded OpenAI in 2015 partly out of fear that AI controlled by Google could be dangerous, still puts the odds of annihilation at about 10 to 20%, and now races to build it at xAI. Source
  • Émile P. Torres. Once wrote a book in defense of longtermist ideas, then left the movement, became one of its most prominent critics, and later co-coined the term TESCREAL with Timnit Gebru. Source
  • Bernie Sanders. Sanders's early AI work centered on jobs and inequality (Oct 2025 HELP report estimating nearly 100 million jobs at risk). By September 2026 he was also treating superintelligence as a threat to humanity and introduced a bill to ban it. Source
  • Nick Bostrom. Bostrom wrote the AI-risk classic 'Superintelligence' (2014), then 'Deep Utopia' (2024) about life after AI solves our problems. In a 2026 working paper he argued that, judged only by the interests of people alive today, even high catastrophe probabilities are often worth accepting to reach superintelligence sooner. Source
  • Peter Thiel. Thiel was an early funder and adviser of Yudkowsky's Singularity Institute (2006). In his 2025 Antichrist lectures he cast calls to stop or regulate AI, including Yudkowsky's, as the work of a 'Luddite' Antichrist figure leading toward a one-world state. Source
